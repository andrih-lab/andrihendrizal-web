In discussions about mangrove management, one question keeps coming up that sounds fair but points the wrong way: which is more right, what residents know or what surveys show? A more useful question is what each one says, where they agree, and where they complete each other.

## Two ways of knowing that overlap

A study in The Bahamas interviewed land-crab harvesters with structured surveys and then compared their answers with the scientific literature (McGaw et al., 2025). The result was not a win for either side. The authors concluded that traditional and scientific knowledge overlap substantially, and that the harvesters' knowledge both supported and expanded what science already knew. One example of agreement: crabs seek cool, moist places beneath objects or among tree roots, in line with earlier research.

The limit should be stated plainly: that study concerned land crabs, not a mangrove community. I use it because its design is clear, document first and then compare, and that design transfers to mangrove settings.

## What surveys cannot reach

Field surveys are costly and limited in time. A study of mullet in northeastern Brazil notes that migration is hard to study because of technological limits and the high cost of long-term monitoring (da Silva et al., 2026). Fishers fill part of that gap. The researchers interviewed mullet-specialist fishers between July 2023 and June 2024 in two sustainable-use protected areas, and mapped 72 sites linked to migration and spawning with them, including mangroves, rivers, sandbanks, coral reefs, and beaches. They judged that the fishers provided precise and complementary information on migration patterns and habitat use.

## Conditions for a fair comparison

Local knowledge is not data that falls from the sky; it has sampling bias too. The mullet study recruited participants by snowball sampling, in which participants name others with similar skills, so those interviewed were only a subset of the local population. That is why the authors stress a three-part combination: structured interviews, participatory mapping, and field validation of species distributions. Without the last step, a knowledge map remains a claim.

Comparison is not only about data. A review of mangrove rehabilitation projects in the Philippines found that science passed down to communities helped initial planning, but long-term community-based management benefited when that knowledge was "localised", meaning it merged with existing knowledge to form hybrid knowledge (Marquez & Olavides, 2024). The study also stresses that continuous local institutional support remains necessary. In Kalibo, for example, a people's organisation was granted a 25-year right to manage the restored mangrove forest.

## Closing

The practical lesson is simple. Record residents' knowledge with a clear method, test it in the field, report what matches and what differs, and do not make either source the judge of the other. Local knowledge is already recognised as a complement to science-based knowledge in restoration and conservation, including in the Paris Agreement (Marquez & Olavides, 2024). The challenge now is not recognition but careful comparison.
