Long-term ecological data is rare in Indonesia's mangrove forests, where most surveys are one-off snapshots: a team visits once, publishes a density or biomass figure for that moment, and moves on. Three sites in Riau — Pangkalan Jambi and Bukit Batu in Bengkalis Regency, and Kelurahan Purnama in Dumai City — are tracked differently, as permanent monitoring sites revisited on a regular cycle rather than surveyed just once.

## What a single survey cannot show

A one-off survey can describe a forest's condition at a point in time, but it cannot distinguish a stand that is degrading from one that is recovering, or tell whether an unusual measurement is a real trend or ordinary year-to-year variation. Those questions only answer themselves with repeat visits to the same fixed plots, measured the same way each time.

At Kelurahan Purnama in Dumai, for instance, the question driving repeat visits is how mangrove density responds to marine debris accumulation along that stretch of coast — a relationship that a single visit could only guess at, since it requires seeing the same plots before and after debris builds up across more than one season. At Pangkalan Jambi in Bengkalis, the same logic applies to a different question: how local ecological knowledge — which species coastal residents recognize as fast-growing, which zones they treat as productive fishing or crabbing ground — shifts as the forest itself visibly changes, which is part of what the DPPM (Directorate of Research and Community Service) grant on Traditional Ecological Knowledge in mangrove management is documenting.

## Two things get revisited, not one

At a permanent site, both the forest plots and the surrounding community get revisited on the same cycle. The ecological side re-measures the same tagged trees for structural change; the social side returns with structured interviews to the same fishers and coastal residents, checking whether what they observe on the ground matches — or gets ahead of — what the plot data shows. Neither half is treated as more authoritative than the other; the point of visiting both repeatedly is to see where local knowledge and field measurements agree, and where the gap between them is itself informative.

## Why it has to be a time series

A single evidence-based management recommendation — where to prioritize replanting, which practices coastal communities already use that are worth formalizing — is much harder to defend from one visit than from a time series. A trend seen across several visits, corroborated by residents who have watched the same stretch of coast for years, carries weight that a single snapshot cannot. That is the case for keeping a small number of sites as permanent monitoring plots even though it means slower, narrower coverage than a one-time regional survey would give.
